When a Tmall or Douyin agency fakes orders, the brand carries most of the
risk. An agency in a case the Supreme People's Court published in August
2026 paid a RMB 100,000 fine and lost its RMB 50,000 deposit. The store's
penalties land on its account holder, which is you. Ban fake orders in
writing and audit the traffic.

Fake orders have a name in Chinese ecommerce: 刷单炒信, brushing orders to
hype a store's credit. An agency that runs your store has every reason to
try it. Most charge a retainer plus a commission on sales, and a ranking
bump is fast.

On August 3, 2026, the Supreme People's Court put one of these cases on a
list of five typical platform cases. The agency lost. The court's summary
says nothing about the merchants whose stores it pumped up. Their exposure
sits in the platform agreements and the law.

We checked the rules and cases below on September 24, 2026. This isn't
legal advice. Have a lawyer qualified in China look at your contract.

## The case: fake group-buy orders and a lost deposit

In February 2021 an agency signed a service-provider agreement with a
platform the court didn't name. It would run stores and marketing for the
platform's merchants in one area, and it paid a RMB 50,000 deposit.

Then it arranged accounts to buy cheap group-buy vouchers that sold nothing
real, and redeemed them, to lift its merchants up the rankings.

> A market regulator found the agency had helped other businesses publish
> false claims by organizing fake transactions, and fined it RMB 100,000.
> The platform docked its integrity score, kept the whole RMB 50,000 deposit
> and cut its back-end access. The Yangpu District court in Shanghai
> dismissed the agency's suit to get the deposit back on September 5, 2025.
> Source: Supreme People's Court (最高人民法院), typical cases on platform business, case 3, August 2026. https://www.court.gov.cn/zixun/xiangqing/507691.html

The judgment, case (2025)沪0110民初8092号, is final. The agency's argument
was that the deposit clause sat in the platform's
standard terms and nobody had pointed it out. The court didn't buy it. The
clause was printed in bold, which it counted as fair notice, and a
professional operator should know fake orders are banned anyway. Keeping
all RMB 50,000 wasn't excessive either, given the damage to the platform's
reputation.

The People's Court Daily, the court system's newspaper, opened its write-up
on the court's own site with the question merchants ask: if a store pays
someone to brush orders, can the platform take a deposit?

Here the deposit was the agency's own. A store's deposit sits under the
store's own agreement, which lets the platform deduct for any breach of the
law.

## How fake orders are run and how platforms catch them

The Yangpu case shows how simple it is. Accounts the agency lined up bought
deals nobody used. On a product marketplace the same trick looks like
friends' accounts buying your goods, empty parcels, a reused tracking
number, or cash-back for a five-star review.

Douyin spells out the list in the revised merchant rule on fake
transactions it published for comment in July 2024. The wording that
matters for a brand is that the third party counts as you.

> Fake transactions include a merchant using third-party hype groups, or a
> third party's tools, services or help. Douyin checks logistics, buyer
> accounts, trading behavior and after-sale requests with big data, judges
> on probability, and treats abnormal orders as fake unless the merchant
> can explain them with evidence.
> Source: Douyin E-commerce (抖音电商), revised merchant rule on fake transactions (虚假交易), July 2024. https://school.jinritemai.com/doudian/web/article/aJADoQgL6cAS

So the burden sits with the merchant. The rule gives you seven days to
appeal a penalty, and it says an appeal won't succeed where a hired
brushing group or tool placed the orders.

Four kinds of bill can follow a fake-order scheme, and the agency doesn't pay
all of them.

> Businesses may not make false or misleading claims about their goods'
> sales or user reviews, and may not help others do so by organizing fake
> transactions or fake reviews. The fine is up to RMB 1 million, or RMB 1
> million to 2 million in serious cases, and the business license can be
> revoked.
> Source: China National Intellectual Property Administration (国家知识产权局), Anti-Unfair Competition Law (revised June 2025, in force October 15, 2025), articles 9 and 25, May 2026. https://www.cnipa.gov.cn/art/2026/5/20/art_104_206437.html

The criminal bar sits higher. The convictions we found were of the people
selling the fake orders.

> Knowingly posting false information online for pay is the crime of
> illegal business operation once turnover reaches RMB 50,000 for a person
> or RMB 150,000 for a company. Knowingly funding such a crime is treated as
> joint crime.
> Source: Supreme People's Court and Supreme People's Procuratorate (最高人民法院 最高人民检察院), interpretation 法释〔2013〕21号, articles 7 and 8, September 2013. http://gongbao.court.gov.cn/Details/2a817c79a6dbd485eb37b8f6fcd7f8.html

> A Hangzhou court sentenced the founder of a fake-order site to five years
> and six months and fined him RMB 900,000, the first such conviction.
> Source: Beijing Youth Daily (北京青年报), carried by China Securities Journal (中证网), June 2017. https://www.cs.com.cn/xwzx/201706/t20170621_5334300.html

## Tmall agency fake orders and brand liability: why the store is yours

If you [run your own store](/insights/china-distributor-vs-own-store)
instead of selling through a distributor, register it to your own company
during [cross-border setup](/enter-china/cross-border-setup) and let the
agency work inside it. That's the right setup. It also means the store's
record is yours.

> Operations, actions and undertakings made through the merchant's Tmall
> Global account are deemed the merchant's, and the merchant alone bears the
> legal consequences.
> Source: Tmall Global (天猫国际), Merchant Service Agreement, clause 5.1, October 2022. https://terms.alicdn.com/legal-agreement/terms/suit_bu1_tmall/suit_bu1_tmall202111051504_43705.html

Tmall's domestic agreement, for stores on Tmall itself, uses almost the same
words.

> All actions completed through a Tmall account are regarded as the
> merchant's own, or as fully authorized by it.
> Source: Tmall (天猫), Merchant Service Agreement, section 3, March 2026. https://terms.alicdn.com/legal-agreement/terms/TD/TD201609271722_89275.html

The sub-account your agency logs in with doesn't change that.

> The seller bears all legal consequences of what it, or anyone it
> authorizes, does through a sub-account.
> Source: Taobao (淘宝), sub-account software license agreement, clause 8.7, June 2023. https://terms.alicdn.com/legal-agreement/terms/suit_bu1_tmall/suit_bu1_tmall201802012028_72666.html

The law points at you too. Article 9 of the Anti-Unfair Competition Law has
two halves: one for the business whose sales and reviews are inflated, and
one for whoever organized the fakes. The agency in the Yangpu case was
fined under the helping half.

Sellers whose own stores are inflated get fined as well.

> In Shanghai's 2025 unfair-competition cases, a company that inflated its
> online store with more than 20,000 fake orders to lift sales and search
> ranking was fined by the Qingpu District market regulator, which also
> referred leads onward.
> Source: China Quality News (中国质量报), on the State Administration for Market Regulation site (市场监管总局), February 2026. https://www.samr.gov.cn/xw/df/art/2026/art_4ab8f37427034d6182dfcbb59482ab6a.html

The E-Commerce Law has said much the same since 2019.

> Ecommerce businesses may not make false or misleading claims through
> fake transactions or invented user reviews.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China (国家网信办), E-Commerce Law (adopted August 31, 2018), article 17, September 2018. https://www.cac.gov.cn/2018-09/01/c_1123362506.htm

Those rules apply to the store, whoever placed the orders.

## What to write into the agency contract

Pick an agency that doesn't need fake orders to look good. Our
[guide to finding a Tmall Partner](/compass/find-a-tmall-partner-in-china)
and our
[guide to finding a Douyin Partner](/compass/find-a-douyin-partner-in-china)
both say to judge work you can open on the platform yourself. A promise to
"buy traffic" is a warning sign on its own.

Then write it down.

- **Ban it by name.** The contract lists fake orders, fake reviews, paid
  cash-back for reviews, bought followers and traffic tools, whether the
  agency does it or a subcontractor does.
- **Price the breach.** Set a fixed penalty per incident. On top of it, the
  agency repays every fine and deposit deduction its fake orders cause, and
  you can end the contract the same day.
- **Keep the keys.** Main account with you, named sub-accounts for the
  agency.
- **Audit monthly, from your own login.** In Business Advisor, Tmall's
  analytics suite, or the shop back end on Douyin, look for orders bunched on one address or
  device, the same buyers coming back for the same item, refunds in batches,
  and conversion that jumps with no campaign behind it.
- **Keep the evidence.** You get seven days to appeal on Douyin. Proof that
  orders were real (customer chats, ad spend, invoices, shipping records)
  is what wins one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can my agency's fake orders get my Tmall store closed?

Yes. Tmall Global's merchant agreement lets the platform remove products,
block search, close the store and freeze or deduct the deposit. It treats acts
through your account as yours, and your agency works through your account.
Whether the agency acted behind your back matters when you sue it. It
doesn't change what the platform does to the store.

### Does the platform's deposit deduction cover my losses?

No. In the Yangpu case the platform kept the agency's own RMB 50,000
deposit, and the court weighed it against the platform's own loss. The
summary says nothing about compensation for the merchants. Your recovery
comes from your contract with the agency, which is why the penalty and
pass-through clauses matter.

### Does any of this apply if my company is outside China?

The platform side does. A Tmall Global store is contracted by the overseas
merchant, and the agreement treats acts through that account as the
merchant's, wherever the company sits. How far a Chinese regulator can reach
an overseas company is a separate question. It's one to put to your lawyer
before it comes up.

### Is buying fake orders a crime in China?

It can be. The 2013 judicial interpretation makes paid posting of
known-false information a crime above set amounts, and treats anyone who
knowingly funds it as an accomplice. Courts have jailed the people who run
fake-order operations. For a brand, the likelier bill is administrative: a
fine that goes on the public credit record, plus whatever the platform does
to the store.
